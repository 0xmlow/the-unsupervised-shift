The strongest giveaways are the repeated assets, simplified street-level detail, and lighting that makes the street feel staged. Values below are starting points at real-world scale.

1. **Repeated, simplified cars — left and middle, along both curbs.** The vehicles read as variations of the same boxy van, with flat rear panels, opaque glazing, and weak wheel detail. Replace the nearest four with distinct, well-modelled vehicles; distant ones can stay simple. Add tyre sidewalls, wheel wells, mirrors, lamp assemblies, and panel gaps of roughly 0.003–0.006 m. Give glass actual thickness and something dark to reflect. Vary parking angles by 1–3° and distance from the curb by 0.1–0.3 m.

2. **Facades look assembled from repeated modules — all three images.** Window openings, brick surfaces, lintels, and fire escapes have conspicuously similar treatment. The buildings lack the accumulated alterations that distinguish neighbouring Manhattan properties. Introduce different window replacements, patched masonry, cables, air conditioners, and blocked openings. Recess glazing 0.12–0.25 m behind the facade; bevel exposed stone edges by 0.003–0.01 m. Concentrate changes on the nearest buildings.

3. **People read as geometric placeholders — especially the lit upper window at right and figures around the pink shop in the middle.** The window occupant has a nearly circular head and rectangular torso; the street figures merge into stiff, poorly articulated shapes. Use convincing posed silhouettes with shoulders, necks, bent elbows, and asymmetrical weight distribution. Move the window figure 0.5–1 m behind the glass, partially occlude it with a curtain, and lower its contrast. Darkness cannot rescue a recognisably primitive outline.

4. **The shop displays are grids of coloured blocks — blue-awning shops in left and middle.** Repeated rectangles with similar saturation and spacing resemble texture placeholders rather than merchandise. Replace the nearest rows with a few recognisable product families, varied labels, recessed shelves, and irregular stocking. Shelf depths of 0.25–0.45 m and overlapping objects will create useful shadows. Reduce saturation on most products and leave some empty spaces.

5. **The wet road has an overly continuous glossy finish — middle, particularly the foreground.** It reads like one coated surface, with fine bump spread evenly across it. Build a large-scale wetness mask: darker damp asphalt, rougher drying patches, and smoother water collected in depressions. Try roughness around 0.65–0.9 for dry aggregate, 0.25–0.45 for damp areas, and 0.03–0.12 for puddles. Keep puddle surfaces flatter than the underlying asphalt.

6. **The street lacks convincing construction and drainage detail — left and middle.** Broad road and pavement surfaces run together too cleanly. The visible crack and isolated covers do not establish a history of repairs. Add irregular utility cuts, patched trenches, curb joints, gutter debris, and storm drains. Give the road a subtle crown of roughly 1–2%, with water collecting near gutters. Use shallow geometry for major patches; reserve bump for aggregate.

7. **The gate mural looks like a graphic applied to a flat wall — right, also visible at left.** Its crisp contours remain unusually uninterrupted across a surface described as a shop gate. Model horizontal shutter slats at approximately 0.06–0.09 m spacing, with shallow overlapping profiles, side tracks, and a bottom rail. Project the existing artwork across that geometry. Add restrained abrasion at slat edges and grime near the ground; avoid uniformly distressing the whole image.

8. **The night lighting is too neatly colour-separated — middle.** Cyan occupies the left foreground and magenta the right like a deliberate lighting setup. The visible fixtures do not clearly explain the extent of those coloured pools. Reduce coloured spill initially by about half, then rebuild it from lights positioned inside the actual shops. Mix in subdued 2700–3500 K interiors and 3500–4500 K street lighting. Let awnings, door frames, and parked vehicles interrupt the illumination.

9. **The illuminated windows behave like flat light cards — all three, clearest at right.** Several have uniformly bright cream panes with little sense of room depth, glass reflection, or window construction. Put simple room boxes 2–4 m behind selected windows, with curtains and lights offset from the openings. Use glass IOR around 1.45 and roughness around 0.03–0.08. Vary room brightness substantially and let curtains create uneven transmission.

10. **The sunrise is too concentrated into a clean, white vanishing point — left.** The luminous opening, long road reflection, and very orderly framing make the light feel designed for the render. Preserve some distant building information inside the glow and reduce any added bloom. Use a sun angular diameter near 0.53° as a baseline, then balance exposure and sky fill before adding haze. Atmospheric perspective should build gradually with distance rather than read as a bright wall at the street’s end.

11. **The cat is an ambiguous tiny prop — middle, just right of centre.** At this scale, its body and legs do not resolve into an immediately believable crossing gait, and its contact with the road is weak. Pose one foreleg forward and the opposite rear leg back, with a natural spinal line and a less rigid tail. Check a shoulder height around 0.23–0.28 m and body length around 0.4–0.5 m. Add a subtle contact shadow and broken reflection only where water actually exists.

12. **The viewpoints feel arranged, and the window viewpoint is not convincing — all three, especially right.** Left and middle share a highly centred, unobstructed street corridor. Right reads closer to a pavement-level architectural view than a photograph taken down from a window. For that shot, place the camera at a plausible height of roughly 4–6 m and aim downward toward the gate; a little sill or jamb can establish the position. For the street views, try a 35–50 mm full-frame equivalent lens at 1.5–1.7 m height, with a modest lateral offset and one incidental foreground obstruction.

The three changes with the best return for the work are:

- **Replace the nearest cars and visible human silhouettes.** A few convincing assets would remove several immediate scale and shape giveaways.
- **Break up road wetness with one well-designed mask.** Uneven dampness, gutter puddles, and rough patches would improve a large part of the middle image.
- **Rebalance the existing lighting.** Reduce cyan and magenta spill, vary window brightness, and recover detail in the sunrise opening before adding more geometry.
