The middle image is closest to a photograph. The left exposes the repeated geometry most clearly. The right has a convincing subject, but the gate artwork, pavement and camera position weaken it. Material and lighting values below are starting points, assuming one Blender unit equals one metre.

1. **The lighting feels arranged around the storefronts.** In the middle, the blue shop, pink shop and street form unusually neat, separate pools of illumination. The right has a conspicuously readable mural beneath a nearly black facade. Reduce broad fill lights and make visible fixtures supply the illumination. Put area lights inside the shops, behind the glazing, with their dimensions matching actual fixtures. Try 3000–3500 K for warm interiors and 4000–4500 K for street LEDs. Reduce the pink light’s saturation and let its spill fall off across the sidewalk.

2. **The road’s material changes too little across large areas.** The left foreground looks uniformly granular and varnished; the middle looks like a continuous hammered, wet surface. Separate dry asphalt, damp asphalt and standing water with irregular masks. Start with roughness around 0.75–0.9 dry, 0.35–0.55 damp and 0.03–0.12 for puddles. Keep aggregate relief around 0.001–0.003 m; larger depressions should be broad and shallow. Concentrate water along gutters, repairs and low spots instead of distributing it evenly.

3. **The cars still read as repeated low-detail assets.** Along both curbs in the left and middle, similar boxy silhouettes, dark windows and simplified front ends make the street feel populated from a small kit. Improve the nearest three cars first: bevel body edges by roughly 0.003–0.008 m, separate tires from wheel arches, add lamp internals and give bumpers distinct materials. Vary parking distance from the curb by 0.1–0.3 m and yaw by a few degrees. Glass needs reflected surroundings plus a readable dashboard or seat silhouette, not just a dark pane.

4. **The facades have too much repeated precision.** In the left especially, window openings, trim, brick tone and floor divisions repeat with little evidence of separate owners or repairs. Introduce variation by building: different window recesses, sill profiles, replacement frames and restrained mortar colours. Recess glazing about 0.12–0.25 m behind the facade where appropriate. Add a few coherent repair zones, runoff below sills and staining near the ground. Random noise across every brick would not solve the larger repetition.

5. **The people lack convincing weight and incidental movement.** The flower heads are a deliberate artistic choice; the giveaway is how the figures beside the middle storefront stand and integrate with the light. Their poses read as staged, with little separation between limbs and torso. Shift weight onto one leg, bend the other knee, rotate shoulders against hips and make hands perform a specific action. Check sole contact and contact shadows. Use cloth roughness around 0.65–0.9 and give petals thickness and restrained translucency so they respond to the shop lighting.

6. **The gate mural looks cleaner and more luminous than its substrate.** In the right image, the cyan contours remain remarkably continuous and readable across the shutter. They compete with the physical relief instead of feeling painted onto it. Use the artwork primarily as base colour, with emission at zero unless it is intentionally luminous. Carry corrugation normals and roughness through the paint. Add narrow paint losses at slat joints, scratches near the bottom and dirt beneath the housing. Keep those marks tied to mechanical wear.

7. **The sunrise ends in a bright, simplified slot.** In the left, the central white opening and straight building recession feel like the end of a constructed set. Add several offset distant building masses and a partially obscured intersection beyond the current street. Use restrained atmospheric depth so successive blocks lose contrast. Try a sun angular diameter of approximately 0.53° and expose for some retained sky colour. Avoid making the entire vanishing point uniformly white.

8. **The crosswalk paint appears too thick and pristine.** In the left, several stripes look like individual slabs, with clean boundaries and dark edges. Put the paint into the road shader or use an almost flush decal; remove any visible slab thickness. Break edges by centimetres, expose asphalt texture through the paint and carry road cracks continuously across it. Add wear along wheel paths. Wet paint can differ in roughness from asphalt without becoming a mirror.

9. **The sidewalks look assembled from clean, chunky blocks.** This is especially visible at the middle corner and across the right foreground. Slab boundaries, curb faces and broad concrete surfaces have too little accumulated damage or drainage logic. Keep typical curb exposure around 0.12–0.18 m, bevel exposed edges by 0.005–0.015 m and vary adjacent slab heights by only a few millimetres. Add gutter sediment, isolated corner chips and darker joints. Check that the storefront thresholds meet the pavement naturally.

10. **The fire escapes remain too uniform in silhouette and material.** In the left and right, repeated rails and platforms merge into clean black structures. Preserve the repaired geometry, but add visible wall anchors, bracket connections and small edge highlights. Check member sizes against plausible construction: rail tubes roughly 0.025–0.04 m in diameter, with thinner infill bars. Use painted-metal roughness around 0.4–0.65, varying locally at joints and exposed edges. Avoid blanket rust or bending every railing.

11. **The small street objects do not have the same finish as the buildings.** In the right, the trash bags read as glossy, crumpled masses; the bin and loose lid feel conspicuously arranged. Give bags distinct filled volumes, stretched areas, tied necks and flattened contact patches. Vary roughness around 0.3–0.6. In the middle, the cat’s small silhouette needs clearer paw contact and a recognisable walking gait. A slight moving-paw blur could help, but only if it matches the chosen shutter speed.

12. **The camera positions feel selected to display the scene.** The left and middle have very similar centred street views, while the right reads closer to a low street-side camera than a photograph from an upstairs window. For the right, establish an actual window height—perhaps 4–6 m—and aim downward enough for the pavement perspective to support it. For one street view, move the camera 0.5–1 m off the centreline and use a slight, deliberate yaw. Keep vertical correction plausible; avoid adding blur or grain as a substitute for geometry and lighting.

The three highest-return changes are:

- Rework the asphalt roughness and puddle masks, and flatten the crosswalk paint.
- Reduce the broad storefront fills and mural brightness so visible fixtures explain the illumination.
- Improve and vary the nearest three cars; they carry far more photographic credibility than the distant parked rows.
